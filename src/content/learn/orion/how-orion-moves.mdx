<p class="intro">
When Orion glances at you, nothing in it thinks "turn the head". A file says which poses to pass through and how long
to take, a program works out a smooth path between them, and five motors follow that path one small step at a time.
This lesson takes those pieces in order. The lamps below run the same motion software as the real Orion.
</p>

<Section>

## Five joints

Orion's body has five places where it can bend or turn. Each one is a <Term id="joint">joint</Term>, and each joint
has its own motor. The base turns the whole arm left and right, the shoulder and elbow raise and fold the arm, and
the head can nod and swivel.

That's all the movement Orion has. Every gesture you'll see, from a yawn to a laugh, is these five angles changing
together.

<Fact>Each joint stops short of its full range, at a <Term id="limit">limit</Term> measured on the real lamp. The sliders stop there too.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
The joints are named `base_yaw_joint`, `shoulder_pitch_joint`, `elbow_pitch_joint`, `head_pitch_joint` and
`head_roll_joint`. "Yaw" means turning around a vertical axis, "pitch" means tipping up and down, and "roll" is the
head's swivel. Their limits live in `hardware/profiles/v2-joints.json`.
</UnderTheHood>

## What's a servo?

Orion's motors are <Term id="servo">servos</Term>. A servo isn't told "spin"; it's told an angle, and it turns there
and holds. Inside, a <Term id="encoder">position sensor</Term> keeps measuring where the output actually is, so the
servo can correct itself and report back.

That's what makes precise movement possible: the software only has to decide which angle each joint should be at,
moment by moment.

<Fact>Orion's servos measure 4096 steps per turn, so each step is less than a tenth of a degree.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
Orion uses Feetech STS3215 serial bus servos: all five share one cable and each has an ID from 1 to 5. Orion sets the
zero of every joint with the lamp folded flat, its rest pose, and converts a reading to an angle with
`(raw − 2047) × 2π / 4096` <Term id="radian">radians</Term>.
</UnderTheHood>

## Poses and motions

A <Term id="pose">pose</Term> is a named set of five angles: `home`, `rest`, `look_left`. A motion is a list of
<Term id="keyframe">keyframes</Term>, each saying "reach this pose, taking this long".

Some motions name poses directly. Others, like a nod, are small changes from wherever the lamp already is, so the same
nod works whether Orion is looking left or right.

<Fact>Orion has 26 named poses and 44 built-in motions, each a short text file.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
This is `look_at_left_expressive`, from `motion/motions/v2/`:

```yaml
motion:
  name: look_at_left_expressive
  space: absolute
  style: expressive_turn
  keyframes:
  - pose: look_left_anticipation
    duration: 0.25
    arrival: through
  - pose: look_left_lean
    duration: 0.4
    arrival: through
  - pose: look_left_overshoot
    duration: 0.3
    arrival: through
  - pose: look_left
    duration: 0.35
    arrival: settle
```
</UnderTheHood>

## Why doesn't Orion jerk?

The simplest way to get from one keyframe to the next is a straight line: move at a steady speed, then stop. But a
straight line starts at full speed and stops dead. That instant change in speed is a jolt: it rattles the arm and makes
the motors clunk.

Orion joins its keyframes with curves instead. Each one starts gently, speeds up, and slows down before it arrives.

<Predict question="The two lamps reach the same poses at the same moments. Which one goes faster at its fastest?" options={["Straight lines", "Orion's curves", "Same speed"]} answer={1}>
Orion's. To leave and arrive gently but still be on time, a curve has to make up the time in the middle. Look at the
peak of the black line in the speed chart.
</Predict>

<UnderTheHood>
Each joint's path between two keyframes is a <Term id="quintic">quintic curve</Term>, a fifth-power polynomial chosen
so the position, <Term id="velocity">speed</Term> and <Term id="acceleration">acceleration</Term> at both ends are
exactly what was asked for. See `runtime/src/motion/trajectory.rs`.
</UnderTheHood>

## Passing through or stopping

Each keyframe says how to arrive. **Settle** means stop there, with no speed left. **Through** means keep moving: the
keyframe is a point to pass, not a place to stop.

A glance left and then right reads as two separate looks when both keyframes settle, and as one sweeping look when the
first passes through.

<Fact>A motion's last keyframe must always settle, so every movement ends at rest.</Fact>

## 50 times a second

The smooth curve is a plan. To follow it, Orion's <Term id="runtime">runtime</Term> runs a
<Term id="control-loop">control loop</Term>: read where each motor is, work out where the plan says it should be
right now, and send the five new angles. Then it does it again, 20 milliseconds later.

Slow the glance down and you can see the separate steps that make up the smooth movement.

<Fact>The loop runs 50 times a second. Slow work such as speech recognition runs elsewhere, so it can never hold the loop up.</Fact>

## Speed limits

A motor can only turn so fast. If a motion asks for a big turn in very little time, it would need a speed the motor
doesn't have.

Orion's <Term id="compiler">compiler</Term>, the part that turns motion files into paths, checks every plan against
that limit. When a move is too fast, it doesn't fail: it makes the move take longer until the speed fits.

<Fact>Orion's servos top out around 312° per second (52 turns a minute).</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
The compiler estimates each segment's peak speed by sampling its curve, stretches the segments that are too fast, and
tries again, up to 12 times. The ceiling is `STS3215_MAX_SPEED_RAD_S`, about 5.45 radians per second.
</UnderTheHood>

## Moving relative to where you are

Most of Orion's small gestures, such as nods, bounces and breathing, aren't written as poses. They're written as small
changes from the current pose, the <Term id="anchor">anchor</Term>: "head up a little, then back". That's why one happy
bounce works wherever Orion happens to be.

There's one catch. Near a joint limit the full gesture might not fit. Rather than clip it, Orion shrinks the whole
gesture evenly, so it keeps its shape.

<Fact>Every relative motion has to end back at its anchor. Orion refuses one that doesn't.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
A relative keyframe resolves to `anchor + style amplitude × scale × offsets`. One `scale` between 0 and 1 is chosen
for the whole motion, the largest that keeps every joint inside its limits.
</UnderTheHood>

## Styles

The same keyframes can be performed calmly or briskly. Every motion names one of eight styles, and the style sets how
it moves: how fast it travels, how much it flows through keyframes, whether the joints move a little out of step, and
how long it takes to settle.

This is how a thinking gesture comes out slow and drifting while a reaction comes out quick and crisp, without
writing every movement twice.

<Predict question="Pick the thinking style. Will the glance take more time or less?" options={["More", "Less"]} answer={0}>
More: thinking has a tempo of 0.68, so every travel time is divided by 0.68 and comes out about half as long again.
</Predict>

## Who gets to move

Many parts of Orion want to move the lamp: idle breathing, listening, speaking, a requested motion, the Stop button.
They can't all move it at once, so movement has an order of priority. A higher priority takes over immediately; a
lower one waits its turn.

The handover is smooth because the new movement starts from exactly where the old one was, and at the same speed.

<Fact>Only the runtime ever sends angles to the motors. Everything else, including voice and Studio, has to ask it ([Meet Orion](/projects/orion/learn/meet-orion/) shows the gatekeeper).</Fact>

## Asked for vs actual

The plan is what Orion asks for, not what happens. A real motor carries a real arm, so it trails the plan slightly and
doesn't quite reach every quick peak.

That's why a movement isn't "done" when its timeline ends. Orion waits until the measured joints have stayed close to
the final pose, which it calls settling.

<Fact>In this recording the head trails the plan by about 60 milliseconds. The shoulder, which carries the whole arm, trails by about 140.</Fact>

## Make your own motion

Now put it together. Pose the lamp with the sliders, add keyframes, choose a style, and press **Compile and play**.
Your motion goes through Orion's own compiler, so the result is exactly what the real lamp would do, including the
messages it gives when it refuses.

When you like it, open "See it as Orion's motion file": that text is a motion Orion can load.

<Fact>The lamp starts every motion from <code>home</code>, just as Orion does when it wakes.</Fact>
