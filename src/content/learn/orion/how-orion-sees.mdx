<p class="intro">
Orion has one wide-angle camera in its head. On its own, a camera only produces a grid of coloured dots. This lesson
follows those dots until they become something Orion can act on: "a person is over there, talking to me", or "your
hand just moved left".
</p>

<Section>

## Finding faces, hands and things

Orion runs three <Term id="detector">detectors</Term> on the camera picture: one for faces, one for palms and one for
everyday objects. Each marks what it finds with a box and a <Term id="confidence">score</Term>. Orion keeps only the
finds that score above a threshold.

Picking that threshold is a trade-off: too strict and it misses people, too loose and it sees faces that aren't there.

<Fact>On the Pi alone, faces are checked 10 times a second. An optional <Term id="accelerator">AI accelerator</Term> raises that to 15.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
The CPU detectors are YuNet (faces), MediaPipe palm detection and YOLO11n (objects); with a Raspberry Pi AI HAT+,
SCRFD, a palm model and YOLOv11n run on the Hailo chip. One worker schedules all three and always uses the latest
frame, dropping older work. Thresholds and rates are in `vision/orion_vision/detector_policy.py`.
</UnderTheHood>

## From a pixel to a direction

A box in the picture only says "this face is at pixel 412". That's no use for turning toward it, because the camera
moves with the head: turn the head and the same face lands on a different pixel.

So Orion records exactly where the camera was pointing when each picture was taken, and turns every find into a
<Term id="bearing">bearing</Term>: a direction from the lamp's base that doesn't change when the head moves.

<Fact>The camera's <Term id="field-of-view">field of view</Term> is about 102° side to side. Pictures are analysed at 640 × 360 pixels.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
Each frame carries its capture time (the middle of the exposure). The runtime supplies the camera's pose at that
instant, worked out from the joint angles and the robot description, so a picture taken mid-turn is still placed
correctly. Detection works in pixels; everything after it works in bearings.
</UnderTheHood>

## Keeping track of who's who

A detector looks at each picture on its own. It doesn't know that the face in this picture is the same person as the
face in the last one. And sometimes it misses a face for a frame or two.

A tracker joins the finds into numbered <Term id="track">tracks</Term>. It predicts where each person will be next, and
a new find close to that prediction keeps the same number. A short miss doesn't lose anyone.

<Fact>A track survives one second without being seen. Finds within 20° of a prediction count as the same person.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
`vision/orion_vision/tracker.py` keeps a constant-velocity Kalman filter per track, in bearings rather than pixels:
each track has a direction and a speed, and every new find nudges both. Track numbers never restart, even after the
camera is switched off and on.
</UnderTheHood>

## Face tracking: choosing whom to look at

With several people in view, Orion has to pick one to look at, and then not flit between them. It sticks with its
current target. It only switches to someone else who is looking at it and is clearly closer, and only once that's been
true for a moment.

## Face tracking: turning to look

Knowing where to look, Orion turns the way people do: the head moves first, because it's light and quick. A moment
later the base starts turning too, slowly, and the head turns back toward the middle as the base takes over. The arm
holds its pose throughout.

Tiny movements are ignored, so Orion doesn't twitch every time someone shifts in their seat.

<Fact>Orion plans a new look every 0.1 seconds. The base joins after 0.2 seconds, and anything within 4° counts as already looking.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
`runtime/src/expression/gaze.rs` solves the head angles against the robot description so the camera points along the
bearing, caps the head's step per update, and compiles each look as a short anchor-relative motion: aim, hold for 1.1
seconds, then return to the anchor. The next update replaces it from the current commanded state, so the movement
stays smooth.
</UnderTheHood>

## Face tracking: who's talking?

When you say "Hey Orion" in a room with other people, Orion turns to you, not to whoever happens to be closest. It
works out who spoke by watching mouths: the face detector also marks points on each mouth, and Orion compares how much
each one moved while the wake word was said.

If it can't tell, it doesn't guess. It would rather not turn than turn to the wrong person.

<Fact>The chosen speaker can stay Orion's target between replies. If they leave, Orion won't swap in a bystander.</Fact>

## Hand tracking

In Hands mode, Orion looks for your palm instead of faces, and follows it. Move your hand left and the head follows,
like a torch you can steer: handy for pointing its light where you're working.

It uses exactly the same turning rules as faces: head first, base after, small movements ignored. When your hand
leaves the picture, Orion holds still for a moment in case it comes back, then returns to its pose.

<Fact>A hand can't wake a resting lamp or switch the motors on. Only a confirmed “Hey Orion” can wake it.</Fact>

## Finding something on request

You can also ask: "look at the mug". Orion runs the object detector, finds the mug, and points its head at it. There
are four possible answers, and Orion is honest about each: it found it; it found it but can't turn that far; it found
several and needs you to choose; or it can't find one.

<Fact>Answers to “look at …” are exactly <code>aimed</code>, <code>clamped</code>, <code>ambiguous</code> or <code>not_found</code>.</Fact>

## Tracking modes and privacy

You decide how much Orion uses its camera. Four tracking modes go from "nothing in the background" to "always follow
faces" or "follow my hand". Separately, a privacy switch turns the camera off completely.

Pictures only ever live in the Pi's memory. They're not saved, and none goes online unless you ask Orion to look at
something.

<Fact>Switching the camera off closes it and clears the images in memory, in every mode.</Fact>
