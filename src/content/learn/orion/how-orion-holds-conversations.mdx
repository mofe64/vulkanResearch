<p class="intro">
Talking to Orion feels simple: you say its name, ask something, and it answers. Underneath, it has to solve a run of
small problems in order. Was that its name? When have you finished? How can it answer quickly? This lesson takes them
one at a time.
</p>

<Section>

## Sound is numbers

A microphone measures air pressure thousands of times a second. Each measurement is one number, a
<Term id="sample">sample</Term>, and the list of them is the recording. Everything Orion does with your voice, it does
to that list of numbers.

<Fact>Orion's microphone board does some of the work itself: it cleans up your voice and removes the sound of Orion's own speaker.</Fact>

</Section>

## Never missing the start

Orion only reacts after it's sure it heard its name, and by then you've already said it. If it only started
recording at that point, it would cut off the start of your sentence.

So it always keeps the last few seconds of sound in memory, overwriting the oldest as it goes. When the
<Term id="wake-word">wake word</Term> is recognised, the moments just before it are still there to use.

<Fact>Orion keeps 3 seconds in memory and adds the 2 seconds before the wake word to the recording.</Fact>

</Section>

## Hearing its name, twice

Listening for "Hey Orion" all day has to be cheap, so the first check is a small, fast detector that compares sound
patterns. It catches the real thing, but also things that merely sound similar.

So anything it flags gets a second check: <Term id="speech-recognition">speech recognition</Term> writes down the first
words, and Orion checks whether they actually say "Hey Orion". Only then does it light up and listen.

<Predict question="Why not use only the second, more accurate check?" options={["It's slower and heavier to run all day", "It can't hear quiet voices"]} answer={0}>
Right. Turning speech into text costs far more computing than matching a sound pattern. The cheap check runs all the
time; the expensive one runs only when there's a reason to.
</Predict>

<UnderTheHood>
The first stage is Rustpotter, trained on recordings of "Hey Orion". The second stage asks Qwen to transcribe a short
prefix of the candidate audio. While it does, the full command keeps recording, so the check costs no time. A rejected
candidate leaves the rolling memory and detector history untouched. See `docs/voice-architecture.md`.
</UnderTheHood>

<div slot="stage"><WakeCheck client:visible /></div>
</Section>

## When have you stopped talking?

Orion has to decide when you're done. A <Term id="vad">voice activity detector</Term> scores each moment from 0 to 1:
how much it sounds like speech. Orion counts speech when the score goes above one line, and silence only when it drops
below a lower one.

The gap between the two lines stops tiny wobbles from flipping it back and forth. After a stretch of silence, the
recording ends.

<Fact>Orion starts counting speech above 0.5, stops below 0.35, and ends the recording after 1.2 seconds of silence.</Fact>

## Cutting the silence

People pause while they think. Before the recording goes to <Term id="speech-recognition">speech recognition</Term>,
Orion trims long pauses down. The <Term id="model">model</Term> then has less audio to work through, so the reply comes sooner, and nothing
you said is lost.

<Fact>Pauses longer than 600 ms become 320 ms. The speech itself is untouched and stays in order.</Fact>

</Section>

## Words, a reply, and a voice

Three programs do the main work. Qwen turns the recording into text on the lamp. Codex, an online
<Term id="language-model">language model</Term>, reads it and writes a reply. Piper,
<Term id="speech-synthesis">speech synthesis</Term> on the lamp, turns the reply into Orion's voice.

To cut the wait, Orion doesn't wait for the whole voice. It starts speaking as soon as the first piece is ready and
keeps making the rest while it talks.

<Fact>If the voice is being made slower than it's spoken, Orion switches to waiting for all of it, so it never stops mid-sentence.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
The coordinator splits the reply text into bounded synthesis jobs. Piper returns numbered audio chunks with an end
marker, which the coordinator checks before uploading them to the runtime for playback. Once a reply has switched to
complete buffering, it stays that way even if later chunks come faster.
</UnderTheHood>

## Your turn again

A conversation isn't one question. After Orion finishes speaking, it waits for the room to go quiet, then listens for a
few seconds without needing its name, so you can just reply.

Sounds and light tell you which stage it's in, so you never have to guess whether it's listening.

<Fact>The follow-up window lasts 5 seconds and shows as a teal pulse on the light.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
Each wake gets a fresh session ID, and a follow-up gets a new ID linked to the finished turn, so a late result from an
old turn can't sneak into a new one.
</UnderTheHood>

## Interrupting Orion

Sometimes you want to cut in. But while Orion talks, its microphones hear its own voice loudest, which would drown
out yours. Orion's microphone board removes its own voice from what it hears, which is called
<Term id="echo-cancellation">echo cancellation</Term>, so a "Hey Orion" said over the reply still gets through.

The wake checks still apply. A sound that merely resembles its name doesn't stop the reply; a confirmed one does.

<Fact>A confirmed interruption ends the old reply and starts a fresh conversation, keeping the words you said just before.</Fact>

<UnderTheHood>
Orion only listens for its name during playback when its acoustic verifier is healthy; the speech-recognition check
alone isn't trusted to interrupt. Interruptions also skip the usual turn toward the speaker, and an alarm being
dismissed takes priority over a new conversation. See "Wake-phrase interruption" in `docs/voice-architecture.md`.
</UnderTheHood>

</Section>

## What stays on the lamp

Your voice is turned into text on the lamp itself. What goes online is the text Codex needs to write a reply, and a
photo only when you ask Orion to look at something.

<Fact>Orion's behaviour journal records what it did, but never camera pixels or microphone audio.</Fact>
